# Problem

A published study article and its LinkedIn post had solid content (a worked example, diagrams,
cited framework claims, a principles section) but no deliberate narrative structure, and the
title named a class-level pattern. A reviewer asked for two changes: structure the posts as
Attention, Interest, Desire, Action "so people are more likely to engage", and position the
title on the highest-level concept the piece credibly covers for the author's target role
(for example an architecture style instead of a class-level pattern). The publishing
guidance only fixed the order of content for a well-known topic (the non-obvious claim first),
so nothing required a hook inside the "see more" cut, a stated problem, the results, or one
clear next step, and nothing told the drafter how high a title could reach.

# Failed Approaches

- Content order alone: the non-obvious claim led, but the post still listed points and links
  with no problem statement, no results line, and no single call to action.
- Titling by the lowest-level concept the example showed: it was accurate and invisible to a
  reader looking for the higher-level concept the piece also covers.

# Solution

Follow `skills/medium-publishing/references/aida-narrative.md` for every article and post.

1. Four stages, in order: Attention (a hook that says why the reader should care), Interest
   (the problem, context, why it matters), Desire (the value, benefits, and results), Action
   (a clear outcome, recommendation, or next step).
2. The angle decides what leads and AIDA decides how the piece moves; the non-obvious claim
   is the Attention.
3. Post: hook inside roughly the first 200 characters, problem in two or three lines, short
   value items with the principles line, one primary link plus one specific question, hashtags
   last. Article: title, subtitle, and first paragraph carry the hook; a "what you get" list
   precedes the body; a closing next-step section ends it.
4. Title and hook name the highest-level concept the body supports, and nothing the body does
   not deliver.
5. Show the stage map with the `NOT POSTED` draft. Retrofit a published piece by editing the
   title, subtitle, opening, and closing in place, leaving code blocks and figures untouched
   and re-verifying their counts and hashes.

# Why

The requirement was knowable (a hook, a problem, value, and a next step are what make a post
engaging) but no acceptance criterion in the flow named it, so a complete, correct draft
passed every gate and still under-performed. A stage map in the approval message turns the
structure into something the reviewer can check at a glance, and the positioning rule keeps a
higher-level title honest by tying it to evidence in the body.
